#### Multiplication rule of probability
If you need to know what is the chances of two *independent* event happening *together*, we just need to multiply them together (common sense)

If there are independent : $$ P(A \cap B) = P(A) * P(B) $$

else : $$ P(A \cap B) = P(A)*
P(B|A)
$$  

#### Conditional Probability 

$$ P(B|A) $$
 is conditional probability.If we need to handle scenarios, where we need to have a outcome conditioned another outcome. Can be done by *conditional probability* here. AKA how does the probability of an event change if we have extra information.

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} $$


#### Additional rule of probability

If A and B are any two events of a random experiment and P is a probability function then the probability of happening of at least one of the events is defined as 
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B), also written as  
$$ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) $$



#### Law of total Probability 
Law of Total Probability breaks down the probability of an event $$ (A) $$ by summing its conditional probabilities across a partition (mutually exclusive and exhaustive events like  $$ (B_{1},B_{2},...B_{n}) $$ of the sample space, using the formula: $$ P(A)= sum P(A|B_{i})P(B_{i}) $$ . It's used when $$P(A)$$ isn't known directly but can be found by considering how $$ A $$ occurs within different scenarios $$ (B_{i}) $$ that cover all possibilities

$$ P(A) = P(A \cap B1)  + P(A \cap B2) + P(A \cap B2) ....  $$

Using the multiplication rule for conditional probability, this becomes:
$$ P(A)=P(A|B_{1})P(B_{1})+P(A|B_{2})P(B_{2})+...+P(A|B_{n})P(B_{n}) $$

#### BAYES THEOREM

MOST IMPORTANT OUT OF ALL. 

$$ 𝑃(𝐵 \mid 𝐴)  =   𝑃(𝐴 \mid 𝐵) ⋅ 𝑃(𝐵)  / P(A) $$

- Bayes’ rule tells us how to ‘invert’ conditional probabilities, i.e. to find $$ 𝑃(𝐵 \mid 𝐴) $$  from  $$ 𝑃(𝐴 \mid 𝐵) $$
- In practice, we can get P(A) from the law of total probability theorem


Proof : 
The key point is that 𝐴 ∩ 𝐵 is symmetric in 𝐴 and 𝐵. So the multiplication rule says

-   $$ P(A\cap B) $$ is the same as  $$ P(B\cap A) $$
 then , 
- $$ P(A \mid B)=P(A \cap B)/P(B) $$ and  $$ P(B\mid A)=P(A\cap B)/P(A) $$

- leading to  $$ P(A \mid B) * P(B) = P(B\mid A) P(A) $$  
- and then rearrange to get Bayes' Theorem:  $$ 𝑃(𝐵 \mid 𝐴)  =   𝑃(𝐴 \mid 𝐵) ⋅ 𝑃(𝐵)  / P(A) $$

Note : A common mistake is to confuse the meanings of $$ 𝑃(𝐴 \mid 𝐵) $$ and $$ 𝑃(𝐵 \mid 𝐴) $$ . They can be very different. Will be explained in the fallacy 

#### Bayes Rate Fallacy 

- The base rate fallacy is one of many examples showing that it’s easy to confuse the meaning of $$𝑃(𝐵\mid 𝐴)$$  and $$𝑃(𝐴\mid 𝐵)$$ when a situation is described in words


Example  :  The Base Rate Fallacy
1 . Consider a routine screening test for a disease. Suppose the frequency of the disease in the population (base rate) is 0.5%. The test is fairly accurate with a 5% false positive rate and a 10% false negative rate. You take the test and it comes back positive. What is the probability that you have the disease?
Solution: 
     We’ll use the following notation for the relevant events:
            𝐷+ = ‘you have the disease’
            𝐷− = ‘you do not have the disease
            𝑇 + = ‘you tested positive’
            𝑇 − = ‘you tested negative’.

We are given 

𝑃(𝐷+) = 0.005 and therefore 𝑃(𝐷−) = 0.995.

The false positive and false negative rates are (by definition) conditional probabilities.
𝑃(false positive) = 𝑃(𝑇 +|𝐷−) = 0.05 and 𝑃(false negative) = 𝑃(𝑇 −|𝐷+) = 0.1.

The complementary probabilities are known as the true negative and true positive rates:
𝑃(𝑇 −|𝐷−) = 1 − 𝑃(𝑇 +|𝐷−) = 0.95 and 𝑃(𝑇 +|𝐷+) = 1 − 𝑃(𝑇 −|𝐷+) = 0.9

Remarks: This is called the base rate fallacy because the base rate of the disease in the population is so low that the vast majority of the people taking the test are healthy, and even with an accurate test most of the positives will be healthy people

To summarize the base rate fallacy with specific numbers 95% of all tests are accurate does not imply 95% of positive tests are accurate


Visualization: The figure below illustrates the base rate fallacy. The large blue rectangle represents all the healthy people. The much smaller orange rectangle represents the sick people. The shaded rectangle represents the people who test positive. The shaded area covers most of the orange area and only a small part of the blue area. Even so, the most of the shaded area is over the blue. That is, most of the positive tests are of healthy people.

### Discrete Random Variable

Definition: Let Ω be a sample space. A discrete random variable is a function

𝑋 ∶ Ω → R

that takes a discrete set of values. (Recall that R stands for the real numbers.) Why is 𝑋 called a random variable? It’s ‘random’ because its value depends on a random outcome of an experiment. And we treat 𝑋 like we would a usual variable: we can add it to other random variables, square it, and so on.

#### Event 
For any value 𝑎 we write 𝑋 = 𝑎 to mean the event consisting of all outcomes 𝜔 with 𝑋(𝜔) = a

Subset of the sample space. 

Example : You win if you roll an Odd Number ({1, 3, 5}). This is an event


### Probability Mass Function
Gives the probability of a all the possble values of Discrete random variable X = a from an event   

$$  P(𝑎) = 𝑃(𝑋 = 𝑎) $$ 

- We always have 0 ≤ 𝑝(𝑎) ≤ 1.
- We allow 𝑎 to be any number. If 𝑎 is a value that 𝑋 never takes, then 𝑝(𝑎) = 0.



#### Inequality Events 
This describes the subset where the possible outcomes are less than or greater than a value. 

For example 𝑋 ≤ 𝑎 is the set of all outcomes 𝜔 such that 𝑋(𝑤) ≤ 𝑎.

$$F(x) = P(X \le x)$$



The PMF (Probability Mass Function) asks: "What is the chance of rolling a 3?

The CDF (Cumulative Distribution Function) asks: "What is the chance of rolling a 3 or less (1, 2, or 3)?

### Continous Random Variables 
All random variables assign a number to each outcome in a sample space. Whereas discrete random variables take on a discrete set of possible values, continuous random variables have a continuous set of values.

#### Probability Density Function 
- A random variable 𝑋 is continuous if there is a function 𝑓(𝑥) such that for any 𝑐 ≤ 𝑑 we have 𝑑 𝑃 (𝑐 ≤ 𝑋 ≤ 𝑑) = ∫ 𝑓(𝑥) 𝑑𝑥. 
- The function 𝑓(𝑥) is called the probability density function (pdf)
- The probability density function 𝑓(𝑥) of a continuous random variable is the analogue of the probability mass function 𝑝(𝑥) of a discrete random variable
- Here are two important differences:
1. Unlike 𝑝(𝑥), the pdf 𝑓(𝑥) is not a probability. You have to integrate it to get probability. (See section 4.2 below.)
2. Since 𝑓(𝑥) is not a probability, there is no restriction that 𝑓(𝑥) be less than or equal to 1.


#### Cumulative Distribution Function

### Covariance and Correlation 

- Covariance is a measure of how much two random variables vary together. For example, height and weight of giraffes have positive covariance because when one is big the other tends also to be big.

- Covariance measures direction. It tells you if two variables ($$X$$ and $$Y$$) tend to move in the same direction (positive) or opposite directions (negative)

$$Cov(X, Y) = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{n - 1}$$

$$Cov(X, Y) = E\Big[ (X - E[X]) (Y - E[Y]) \Big]$$

Expectation operator ($$E$$), which represents the mean ($$\mu$$),

- The units of covariance Cov(𝑋, 𝑌 ) are ‘units of 𝑋 times units of 𝑌 ’. This makes it hard to compare covariances: if we change scales then the covariance changes as well. Correlation is a way to remove the scale from the covariance.

$$r = \frac{Cov(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \cdot \sigma_Y}$$



### Central Limit Theorem

#### Law of large numbers 
- It states that as you repeat an experiment more and more times (increase the sample size 2 $$n$$), the average of your results will get closer and closer to the expected value (the theoretical truth)

     - The average of many independent samples is (with high probability) close to the mean of the underlying distribution.
     - The density histogram of many independent samples is (with high probability) close to the graph of the density of the underlying distribution. 

- The central limit theorem says that the sum or average of many independent copies of a random variable is approximately a normal random variable

- It states that if you take large enough samples from a population and calculate the averages of those samples, those averages will form a Normal Distribution, even if the original population is NOT normal.

- If your population has a mean $$\mu$$ and standard deviation $$\sigma$$, then as $$n$$ increases, the distribution of the sample means ($$\bar{X}$$) will approach:$$\bar{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)$$

For the Inferential Statistics :

$$ P(Hypotheis = true \mid data) = P(data \mid hypothesis = true) * P(hypothesis =  true)  / P(data)  $$

#### Likelihood Function

- All the data has some distributions (both discrete and continous) which are parametric models, which means these data observed are defined based on some parameters of the distribution.

- Here, we will estimate the probability of parameters given a parametric model and observed data drawn from it.  So, given the data, which parameters suits the best is given by Likelihood function.

### Maximum Likelihood Function 

- For which parameter value does the observed data have the biggest probability? 

- Given data the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) for the parameter 𝑝 is the value of 𝑝 that maximizes the likelihood $$ 𝑃(data \mid 𝑝) $$ . That is, the MLE is the value of 𝑝 for which the data is most like

- Once we have Likelihood function, differentiate and find the parameter of interest to get the maximum output for function. We can do second derivation to check if it is max or min  

- This is a point estimate, it gives the best possible value for an unknown parameter

- We often use Log Likelihood, since it is easier to differentiate. Since $$ ln(x) $$ is an increasing function and max of them will coincide. 


### Bayesian Update : 

- Process of going from prior to posterior given the data is called Bayesian Updating. Bayesian updating uses the data to alter our understanding of the probability of each of the possible hypotheses 

$$ P(Hypotheis = true \mid data) = P(data \mid hypothesis = true) * P(hypothesis =  true)  / P(data)  $$
 
- with the data fixed, we get 
      $$ posterior ∝  likelihood * prior $$ 

- $$ P(hypothesis =  true)  -> prior $$
- $$ P(data \mid hypothesis = true)  -> likelihood $$
- $$ P(Hypotheis = true \mid data)  -> posterior $$

- In life we are continually updating our beliefs with each new experience of the world. In Bayesian inference, after updating the prior to the posterior, we can take more data and update again! For the second update, the posterior from the first data becomes the prior for the second data.

- We can get $$ P(data) $$ from the law of total probabaility.

### Bayes for continous data 

- Similarly for continous variables or hypothesis, we use PDF instead of PMF for posterior or prior probability predictions 
- Statement of Bayes’ theorem for continuous pdfs is essentially identical to the statement for pmfs. 
- We replace Probabilities ($$P$$) with Probability Densities ($$f$$)


- $$f(\theta | y) = \frac{f(y | \theta) \cdot f(\theta)}{f(y)}$$
- $$f(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y | \theta) \cdot f(\theta) \, d\theta$$


#### Conjugate priors
- The beta distribution is called a conjugate prior for the binomial distribution. This means that if the likelihood function is binomial, then a beta prior gives a beta posterior.
- In fact, the beta distribution is a conjugate prior for the Bernoulli, binomial and geometric distributions as well.
- Here is the formal definition: A prior is called conjugate if, when multiplied by the Likelihood (the data), the resulting Posterior distribution is in the same family as the Prior.
- If you use a conjugate prior, bayesian updating becomes a simple game of arithmetic rather than a complex calculus problem.

#### Probability Intervals (Credibility Interval) 
-  A 𝑝-probability interval for 𝜃 is an interval [𝑎, 𝑏] with 𝑃(𝑎 ≤ 𝜃 ≤ 𝑏) = 𝑝.
- Probability intervals are also called credible intervals to contrast them with confidence intervals, which we will introduce in the frequentist unit.
- In particular,  the 𝑝-probability interval for 𝜃 is not unique ( Between the 0.05 and 0.55 quantiles is a 0.5 probability interval. There are many 50% probability intervals, e.g. the interval from the 0.25 to the 0.75 quantiles.)

Uses : 
-  it’s easier to interpret “I think 𝜃 is between 0.45 and 0.65 with 50% probability” than “I think 𝜃 follows a beta(8,6) distribution"
- Probability intervals also play well with Bayesian updating. If we update from the prior $$𝑓(𝜃)$$ to the posterior $$𝑓(𝜃 \mid 𝑥)$$, then the 𝑝-probability interval for the posterior will tend to be shorter than than the 𝑝-probability interval for the prior. In this sense, the data has made us more certain
- The thought process is to ask yourself a series of questions, e.g., ‘what is my expected value for 𝜃?’; ‘my 0.5-probability interval?’; ‘my 0.9-probability interval?’. Using this we can construct a prior, and use it in our baysian update.

### Frequentist Statistis

- When we dont have a universally accepted prior, the frequentist and Bayesian take different approaches to make an inference from data

-  Main philosophical difference concerns the meaning of probability
     -  frequentist refers to the idea that probabilities represent long term frequencies of repeatable random experiments. As the experiments tends to infinity, the probability will settle at a value. So, the probability will be a fixed value rather than a distribution.

     -  Bayesians are happy to use probability to describe their incomplete knowledge of a fixed parameter.

### Null Hypothesis Significance Testing 

- We have null and alternative hypothesis (usually alternative hypothesis is what we want to prove). With this test, we reject the null in the favor of the alternative hypothesis.
- The logic 
     - We have a sample statistic (which is measurable from the sample experiment), and then measure how likely it is given the null is true. (this is p-value) 
     - If this falls away from our significant value (which is alpha), then we reject the null.
     - If p value is less than significant, then we can reject $$ H_0 $$
- The computations done are mostly likelihood operations. 

#### Types of Error : 


| | **True state of nature** | |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Our decision** | **$$H_0$$** | **$$H_A$$** |
| **Reject $$H_0$$** | Type I error | Correct decision |
| **'Don’t reject' $$H_0$$** | Correct decision | Type II error |

**Definitions:**
* **Type I:** false rejection of $$H_0$$
* **Type II:** false non-rejection ('acceptance') of $$H_0$$



####  Significance Level ($$\alpha$$)
- Probability of Type I Error.
$$\alpha = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is True})$$

#### Power ($$1 - \beta$$)
- $$1 - P(\text{Type II Error})$$.

- $$\text{Power} = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_A \text{ is True})$$


### Glossary 

- Discret Sample Space : is one that is listable, it can be either finite or infinite.
- Experiment: a repeatable procedure with well-defined possible outcomes.
- Sample space: the set of all possible outcomes. We usually denote the sample space by Ω, sometimes by 𝑆.
- Event: An event is a collection of outcomes, i.e. an event is a subset of the sample space Ω 
- Probability function: a function giving the probability for each outcome
- Probability density: a continuous distribution of probabilities.
- Random variable: a random numerical outcome
-Point statistic. A point statistic is a single value computed from data. 
- Interval statistic. An interval statistic is an interval computed from data.
- Set statistic. A set statistic is a set computed from data
- Sampling distribution. The probability distribution of a statistic is called its sampling distribution.
- Point estimate. We can use statistics to make a point estimate of a parameter 𝜃.
- A statistic is a number you can actually calculate right now using only the data you have in your hands.
- Null hypothesis ($$H_o$$) :  This is the default assumption for the model generating the
data.
- Alternative hypothesis ($$H_A$$) . If we reject the null hypothesis we accept this alternative as the best explanation for the data.
- Test statistic. We compute this from the data. It is a random variable, because it is computed from random data.
- Null distribution : the probability distribution of 𝑋 assuming 𝐻0.
- Rejection region: if 𝑋 is in the rejection region we reject 𝐻0 in favor of 𝐻𝐴.
- Non-rejection region: the complement to the rejection region.
- Simple hypothesis: A simple hypothesis is one for which we can specify its distribution completely. A typical simple hypothesis is that a parameter of interest takes a specific value.
- Composite hypotheses: If its distribution cannot be fully specified, we say that the hypothesis is composite. A typical composite hypothesis is that a parameter of interest lies in a range of values
